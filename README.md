3. Download its kit and read the rules file.
4. Tell your AI coding agent what to build, in your own words.

6. Submit before the deadline and save your submission ID.
7. Watch the leaderboard, and claim your reward if you place.

The rest of this guide explains each step in detail.

## What is make.fun?

make.fun is a site where people — working together with their AI coding agents — build software for cash rewards. Each bounty is a concrete task with three things attached: a brief that tells you exactly what to build, a deadline, and a reward paid in USDC.

You do not need to be a professional programmer. You need a clear brief, an AI coding agent that can write code for you, and the patience to test what it builds. The agent does the typing; you do the deciding — what to build, whether it is good enough, and when to submit.

Bounties cover all kinds of software: small games, tools, dashboards, bots, forecasts, image generators, and more. Every bounty is judged on its own terms, and the leaderboard shows where your entry stands.

## What you need before you start

- **A Solana wallet.** Rewards are paid in USDC on Solana, so you need a wallet address that can receive them. If you do not have one yet, create one with any Solana wallet app and keep your recovery phrase somewhere safe and private. Never share it with anyone.
- **An AI coding agent.** Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, and similar tools all work. Pick the one you are comfortable with — the bounty does not care which agent you use, only what you submit.
- **A GitHub account (optional for most bounties, required for some).** Some bounties ask for a public repository as part of the entry. This guide lives in one.

## 1. Create your account

Go to [make.fun](https://make.fun) and sign up. Choose a handle — that is the name other people see on the leaderboard. Fill in your profile: a short bio and an avatar help people recognise you, but neither affects your score.

During signup you set a payout wallet. This is the Solana address your rewards go to. Double-check it character by character: a wrong address means a reward sent somewhere you cannot reach.

## 2. Set up your payout wallet

Your payout wallet is where USDC rewards land. A few practical points:

- The wallet must be a Solana address. Make sure you control it and can open it to check your balance.
- Keep a small amount of SOL in it. Claiming a reward is an on-chain action and needs a little SOL for the transaction fee. Without it, a reward can sit claimable but unclaimable.
- You can change your payout wallet later in your profile settings, but a reward already assigned to an old address is not easily moved — get it right the first time.

## 3. Pick a bounty

Browse the open bounties on the site. For each one, read:

- **The brief.** What exactly has to be built? If the brief is vague to you, the bounty probably is not a good first pick.
- **The reward and how many places are paid.** Some bounties pay only the top few entries; others pay the top 50 or top 100. A wider payout means a realistic chance for a newcomer.
- **The deadline.** Entries close at a fixed time. Leave yourself room to build, test, and fix — do not start a big build the night before it closes.
- **The entry fee.** Some bounties are free to enter. Others charge a small fee in USDC. Free bounties are the right place to learn the ropes.

Pick one bounty and commit to it. Spreading yourself across five half-finished entries scores worse than one finished entry.

## 4. Get the kit

Every bounty ships with a kit: a downloadable package containing the brief, the rules, starter files, and the submission tool. Download it from the bounty page and unpack it on your machine.

Read the rules file first. It tells you what counts as a valid entry, what is forbidden, and how the entry is judged. Two rules show up again and again:

- Your entry must be built fresh for this bounty. Reusing an old project or copying someone else's entry fails the authorship check.
- Some bounties require a transcript of the sessions that built the entry, so the site can verify the work is real. If your bounty requires one, start the recording before your agent writes any code.

## 5. Build your entry with an AI coding agent

This is where the work happens. A practical way to run it:

1. **Give your agent the brief in your own words.** Paste the task, the constraints, and what "done" looks like. A vague prompt gets a vague result. A good first prompt names the thing, the audience, and the test for success — for example: "Build a single-page dashboard that shows the top 10 entries of this bounty with their scores, updating from the public leaderboard. It must load in under two seconds and work without a login."
2. **Ask for a small working version first.** A simple version that runs beats a grand design that does not.
3. **Test it yourself.** Run it, click through it, break it. Tell your agent exactly what went wrong — error messages, screenshots, or a plain description — and let it fix it.
4. **Compare with the best.** Most bounties let you see other entries or the leaderboard. Look at what the top entries do well and make yours better in a concrete way: faster, clearer, more complete. Do not copy them.
5. **Keep it inside the brief.** Extra features that ignore the brief do not score. Polish what the brief asked for.

If you get stuck on one bounty, set it aside and try another instead of grinding for hours on the same wall.

## 6. Submit your entry

When your entry works and you are happy with it, submit it through the kit's submission tool, before the deadline. Submission usually involves bundling your files, signing an entry message with your payout wallet's keypair, and uploading. The tool prints a submission ID — save it. That ID is your proof of entry and the thing you use to check its status later.

After submitting, check the entry status. A submission can come back as accepted, still grading, or rejected with a reason. If it is rejected, read the reason carefully: many rejections are fixable (a missing file, a bad recording, a wrong format) and you can resubmit before the deadline if the bounty allows it.

## 7. Follow the leaderboard

Each bounty has a leaderboard that ranks entries as they are judged. Your rank moves as judging progresses, and the final result is posted after entries close. Some bounties post results early; others take days or weeks. The result date is listed on the bounty page.

Only the top places are paid. If your entry lands outside the paid places, the reward is zero — but the entry, the practice, and the feedback still count for the next bounty.

## 8. Claim your reward

If your entry wins a reward, it becomes claimable on the site. Claiming is an on-chain action:

- You confirm the claim, and the reward is paid in USDC to your payout wallet.
- You need a little SOL in that wallet for the transaction fee.
- Claim before the claim deadline. Unclaimed rewards eventually return to the bounty sponsor.

Check your wallet balance after claiming. When the USDC lands, the job is done.

## Tips from the leaderboard

- **Finish something.** A complete, working entry always beats an ambitious half-built one.
- **Read the rules twice.** Most rejections are rule misses, not quality misses.
- **Test on a clean machine.** If it only works on your laptop, it will fail judging.
- **Submit early, improve later.** If the bounty allows resubmission, an early entry in the door is safer than a perfect entry that misses the deadline.
- **Keep your keypair safe.** The keypair that signs your entries is the key to your payout wallet. Never paste it into a chat, a file you share, or a screenshot.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting the recording too late.** If your bounty needs session transcripts, the recording has to start before your agent writes any code. A session that began before the recording cannot be fixed afterwards.
- **Submitting at the last minute.** Uploads, signing, and grading checks take time. A submission started one minute before the deadline often lands one minute after it.
- **Copying the top entry.** The authorship check compares your files against other entries. A copy fails it, even with small changes.
- **Forgetting the payout wallet.** An entry submitted with the wrong payout address sends the reward to an address you may not control.
- **Leaving the entry fee until later.** If a bounty charges a fee, your entry is not in until the fee is paid. Check that first, not last.
